If you only have 5 minutes

The five ideas that came up again and again, in plain language.

  1. Most AI pilots never make it to real use — and it's rarely the AI's fault. The most-repeated number of the summit: roughly 80% of AI projects never reach full production. The real blockers were messy/scattered data, tools bolted onto old workflows instead of built into them, and — most often — no senior person willing to approve scaling something up.
  2. Picking the AI model is the easy part now. Every major AI company (Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft, AWS) is now selling hands-on help to actually deploy AI inside a company, not just software access. The competition has moved from "whose model is smartest" to "who can actually get it running."
  3. Robots and physical AI need a stricter safety rule than chatbots. A chatbot's wrong answer is annoying; a robot's wrong action can hurt someone. The clearest rule offered: let AI act on its own only where a mistake can be undone. Anything that can't be undone stays a human decision.
  4. Good results always traced back to good data — not a fancier model. Storage companies, database companies, and AI labs all said the same thing in different words: get your data organized and accessible first, or nothing built on top of it will work reliably.
  5. The workforce most comfortable with AI is also the newest. Roughly 30% of the global office workforce is now Gen Z — the group most likely to already use AI tools daily. Training and change-management plans built only for senior staff will miss where AI habits are actually forming.

Six terms worth knowing before you read on

Skip this if you're already fluent in AI-conference jargon. Otherwise, these six terms show up repeatedly below.

Agentic AI — AI that doesn't just answer a question, but takes multi-step actions on its own toward a goal (e.g. booking, researching, filing).
AX — shorthand used throughout the summit for "AI Transformation," the enterprise-wide effort to adopt AI, not just a single tool.
Harness Engineering — the tooling and guardrails built around an AI model so it can be trusted to run unsupervised (the difference between a weekend prototype and something IT will actually support).
Sim-to-Real gap — when something trained or tested in a simulator/lab works, then fails once it meets real-world mess (bad lighting, heat, unpredictable people).
World model — an AI's internal understanding of how physical things behave (gravity, collisions, cause and effect) — the piece robots need that chatbots don't.
FDE (Forward-Deployed Engineer) — a vendor's own engineer embedded inside a customer's rollout, rather than just selling a license and walking away.

Day 1 — Foundations & the enterprise reality check

The first day moved from where agentic AI came from, to how far it actually reaches into production today — with two recurring warnings: most AI pilots never scale past a demo, and the physical/robotics wave ("Physical AI") is arriving faster than the safety testing built for it.

Morning · Main Stage

The Long Road to Agentic AI: A Pioneer's Perspective 선구자의 관점에서 본 AI 어시스턴트에서 에이전틱 AI의 시대

Speaker name not captured on slide Career: SRI → Nuance → Yahoo → Cortana → Google Assistant → Samsung Bixby/Viv → Georgia Tech

In short: Today's AI assistants are less controllable than the voice assistants of the 2010s — and the fix on offer is a smaller, checkable control layer, not a bigger model.

  • Traced "agentic AI" back to SRI's 1995 Office Assistant — a system that routed speech, email, and calendar requests to specialist agents, the direct ancestor of Siri and of what we now call agentic AI.
  • Argued 2010s-era assistants (Cortana, Google Assistant, Bixby) were deliberately limited, but controllable and fixable when something went wrong.
  • Called 2022's ChatGPT moment "the wrong turn": fluent, but the older, more controllable structure was thrown out along with the friction.
  • Cited 2026 research showing today's agents guess when a task is unclear (89% success with a clear task vs. 6.7% with a vague one) and get stuck repeating their own wrong assumption in longer conversations.
  • Proposed a fix: a much smaller "sidecar" system that checks the model's actual reasoning (not just its final words), plus better real-time back-and-forth — "a meeting of the minds," not more autonomy.
TakeawayFluency isn't the same as reliability. The field spent a decade building controllable systems, then reset to something harder to steer when today's AI arrived.
Morning · Main Stage

Agentic AI in Space: Traditional and Agentic AI Are Revolutionizing Space 지구 밖의 자율 AI: 60개 위성이 스스로 생각하고 협력하는 법

Speaker name not captured on slide

In short: A system that automatically points ~60 satellites at a developing event (like a volcano) — humans set the goal, the system handles the busywork.

  • Demoed a workflow that detects an event (e.g. a volcanic heat signature), automatically picks the right satellites to look at it, and tracks it — shown live with a simulated Mount Pinatubo eruption.
  • Framed the goal as "humans on the loop, not in the loop" — people set intent and check results; the system does the coordination.
  • Closed on the idea of AI that builds its own step-by-step plan from a goal, instead of engineers hand-building a plan for every new situation.
Takeaway"Set the goal, check the result" is a clean pattern for anything that has to watch and coordinate many sensors or systems at once — on Earth or off it.
Morning · Main Stage

Is AI Actually Helping Enterprise Productivity?

Featuring Prof. Kyungjong Lee — KB Kookmin Bank / KB Financial Group

In short: Companies are adopting AI fast, but very few can show it actually helped their bottom line.

  • Of executives running multiple AI projects, only 6% said the projects moved profit by more than 2% — 94% saw no measurable financial impact.
  • A Bank of Korea survey found 60–70% of Korean companies say they're adopting AI, but fewer than 10% report actual results.
  • Gartner data: over half of enterprise AI projects get cancelled at the "just testing it" stage, before ever reaching real use.
  • KB Financial Group described its own path: its AI-agent platform started development in 2024, launched in April 2025, and is now scaling up.
Takeaway"Everyone's using AI" and "AI is actually working here" are two very different claims — and the gap between them was this summit's biggest running theme.
Midday · Main Stage

2026 Enterprise Agentic AX Technology Trends: Harness & Loop Engineering 2026 Enterprise Agentic AX 기술 트렌드 및 Harness & Loop 엔지니어링 적용 사례 소개

Speaker name not captured on slide

In short: There's now an entire discipline dedicated to making AI safe and reliable to run day-to-day — and every major vendor is racing to sell that layer, not just the model.

  • Described a maturity ladder — casual chatbot use, then simple developer tools, then multi-tool platforms, then fully governed enterprise setups — with a clear warning: the easier/faster end of that ladder is also the hardest to control.
  • Explained "Loop Engineering": instead of a person prompting the AI step by step, you design a small loop — set a goal, let the agent try, have it check its own work, fix on failure, stop on success — with a human checking in on the bigger picture, not every step.
  • Covered tools that give AI agents a working "memory" across conversations, which most AI systems still lack by default.
  • Closed on a striking fact: Anthropic, OpenAI, AWS, and Microsoft have together committed over $9 billion combined to embedding their own staff inside customers' AI rollouts.
TakeawayEvery major AI vendor is now selling hands-on deployment help, not just model access — proof that the hard part was never picking a model.
Afternoon · Main Stage

The Agentic Data Stack: Speed and Simplicity for AI That Scales

ClickHouse

In short: AI agents hammer a database with far more unpredictable requests than a normal app does — you need a database built for that pattern.

  • Walked through how AI queries databases changed over 2022–2025: from one predictable request per user, to an AI agent firing off many unpredictable requests at once.
  • Shared that OpenAI's daily log data is comparable to "500 Libraries of Congress a day," growing over 20% a month — and that Anthropic credited this same database technology as instrumental in shipping Claude 4.
TakeawayAI agents don't just need "a database" — they need one built for many unpredictable requests at once, not the steady, predictable pattern older software assumes.
Afternoon · Main Stage

From Models to Reality: What It Takes to Run AI Reliably at Scale 모델에서 현실까지: 대규모 AI를 안정적으로 운영하기 위한 조건

Colin Presly · VP, Customer Engineering, Seagate Technology

In short: AI made data itself the scarce, valuable resource — and most of it still ends up sitting on plain hard drives, not fancy chips.

  • Described three stages most companies pass through: can we build something useful → can we run it in production → does it create lasting value. Most companies are still stuck between the first two.
  • Cited data-growth estimates: under 1 zettabyte of data existed in 2005, 72 in 2020, and a projected 527 by 2029 — with AI training and AI's day-to-day use creating two different, both-growing kinds of demand for storage.
  • Noted that 85–95% of all AI data ultimately rests on ordinary hard drives, not premium chips — making basic storage density and cost a real lever on AI's overall cost and environmental footprint.
TakeawayEvery AI conversation eventually becomes a data-storage-and-governance conversation. The model gets the attention; where the data actually lives decides whether it's affordable and trustworthy.
Afternoon · Main Stage

How AI Is Redefining the Future of Work AI가 바꾸는 일의 미래

Speaker name not captured on slide

In short: The newest, youngest part of the workforce is also the most AI-fluent — plan training around that reality, not around senior staff alone.

  • Roughly 30% of the global office workforce is now Gen Z, a group that shows notably higher entrepreneurial and side-project instincts than earlier generations.
  • Noted a Korea-specific detail: mandatory military service and long job-hunting periods push back the age Korean Gen Z workers actually start full-time roles, compared to other countries.
TakeawayAI habits are forming fastest at the newest, least senior level of the workforce — governance and training built only for leadership will miss it.
Late afternoon · Keynote

Building as a Creative Act: An Innovator's Mindset for the AI Age AI 시대의 혁신가의 마인드셋

Savannah Kunovsky · Managing Director, IDEO AI MTechLab

In short: This talk's idea of "embodiment" — AI that has to act in the physical world, not just answer questions — became the thread that connected the rest of the day.

  • Opening keynote of the afternoon, framed as "the innovator's mindset in the age of AI" from IDEO's AI-focused lab.
  • Its framing of embodiment was picked up directly, by name, later that same day in the Physical AI panel.
Takeaway"Embodiment" — intelligence that has to act in, and be checked against, the physical world — was this summit's connective thread between design thinking and hard robotics/defense discussions.
Late afternoon · Main Stage

Beyond Better Models: What Will Define the Next Era of AI? AI 에이전트의 미래: 더 나은 모델이 아니라 더 나은 워크플로우

Genspark

In short: A second speaker independently landed on the same idea as the morning's opening talk — the real unlock is memory/context, not a bigger model.

  • Shared growth numbers as evidence, not boasting: $250M in yearly revenue within 12 months from zero, $645M raised at a $2.6B valuation, over 7,000 paying business customers.
  • Core message: "better models will keep shipping — the breakthrough is context." Described a "second brain" layer that follows a person across apps, rather than living inside any single tool.
TakeawayTwo unrelated speakers, hours apart, reached the same conclusion independently: a persistent memory/context layer matters more than a bigger model.
Evening · Keynote Panel

The Era of Physical AI: The Future of Industry Transformed by Physical AI

Moderator: Prof. Doohee Jeong — Handong Global University / Impactive AI Prof. Ahyoung Kim — Seoul National University Tony Jang — CEO, FactoryLab.AI (ex-Hyundai AutoEver) CTO Seung-young Lee — LIG Defense & Aerospace

In short: The single most useful idea from the whole summit: let AI decide freely only where a mistake can be undone — everything else stays a human call.

  • Why now: The turning point traced through AlphaGo → an earlier wave of AI that disappointed robotics (2014–2020) → the technology behind ChatGPT, which succeeded in language, then vision, then robotics — the shift from just sensing the world to actually understanding it.
  • Demo vs. real deployment — three questions (Tony Jang): Does using it require changing how things already work? Does a failure fit inside normal turnaround time? Is the data needed to keep it accurate clearly someone's job? If all three check out, it's real — not just "a cool demo."
  • When is a "world model" real, not marketing? A simulator built on real physics is proven technology; a system claiming to perfectly understand physics from scratch is "still at the packaging stage." A described incident at Tesla — its Optimus robot misidentifying a coworker as a part and pinning them to a wall — was cited as the real cost of getting this wrong.
  • Where should AI's freedom to act stop? (CTO Seung-young Lee, defense context): the line is whether the outcome is reversible, not how smart the AI is. Where a decision can't be undone, AI's job is to hand humans better information faster — not to decide. That's a deliberate boundary, not a current technical limit.
  • Why lab-tested AI often fails in the real world: roughly half the time it's a software issue (a lab's lighting/conditions don't match reality); the other half is hardware (real heat, vibration, and signal loss that's hard to simulate at all).
  • Testing under active interference, not just noise: in defense, the threat isn't accidental error — it's deliberate jamming and deception. The proposed fix: test against a wide range of realistic conditions, keep expanding that range as new edge cases turn up, and treat this as something that needs government-level involvement, not one company solving it alone.
TakeawayUse reversibility, not how capable the AI seems, as the line for how much it's allowed to decide — and test any physical AI system against a wide range of real conditions, not a fixed checklist, before trusting it in the field.
Evening · Main Stage

The Rise of AI-Native Work AI 네이티브 워크플로우 혁명

Laurent Thevenet — Head of Creative Technology, APAC & MEA, Publicis Groupe

In short: Give AI users a shared, supported toolkit instead of restricting them — and grow the ownership of a tool as its use grows.

  • "Instant capability isn't expertise": AI gives you fast output, but a person still needs to judge whether it's actually good and what could go wrong — "the gap is slop."
  • Warned against treating "which model to use for which task" as a strategy on its own — it's plumbing, not transformation.
  • Argued the fix for ungoverned tool sprawl isn't restricting people — it's giving them a shared, well-supported set of tools and data to build on.
  • Proposed a simple rule for when a tool needs formal ownership: 5 users, the builder still owns it fine; 50 users, it needs a named owner; 500 users, it needs a full team.
TakeawayA simple ready-made rule for when an internal AI prototype needs formal ownership: 5 users → the builder; 50 → a named owner; 500 → a full team.

Accelerating Breakthroughs with AI: Unlocking Opportunities in Traditional Industries

Ramine Tinati — Head of APAC, Google DeepMind Moderator: Seri Kim

In short: Most AI pilots fail as a systems problem (people, data, workflow) — not because the AI itself is weak.

  • Opened with DeepMind's science-first roots (the AlphaGo match, played in Seoul a decade ago), then its non-chatbot track record: AlphaFold (200M+ protein structures, now free even for commercial use), a materials-science model with 2.2M predicted crystal structures, and weather-forecasting tools.
  • Repeated the central statistic: roughly 80% of AI projects never reach full production — a systems problem spanning people, data, existing tools, and infrastructure, not a "the model isn't smart enough" problem.
  • Why pilots stall — three causes: scattered/unusable data, AI added on top of a workflow instead of built into it, and cultural resistance (no one senior owning it).
  • Four fixes: get the data solid and verified first; keep re-checking the AI against how your industry actually talks; build AI into daily tasks, not just chat; and put real safety/oversight checks in place.
  • Named three industries with the most room to benefit from this approach: healthcare/life sciences, manufacturing/supply chain, and telecom/infrastructure.
TakeawayThe summit's most-repeated number — 80% of AI projects don't reach production — traces back to the same root cause every time: data readiness and how well it's built into daily work, not the AI's raw capability.

Panel: Hidden Bottlenecks in Enterprise AI — Data Quality, Governance, and Reality

Moderator: name unconfirmed ("Seunghyun"/"Seunghwan" Lee), VP, Samil PwC Yongmin Choi — Head of AI Strategy Center, Woori Financial Group Yoonhee Hwang — Senior VP, Data Intelligence, LG Electronics

In short: The single biggest blocker wasn't the technology — it was nobody senior willing to say "yes, scale it up."

  • Named the real bottleneck of the last 2–3 years as that exact moment — someone senior has to approve scaling a pilot that already works.
  • Used a gold-rush comparison: in past tech waves, the money went to the companies selling the infrastructure (chipmakers, cloud providers), not most of the people trying to use it — a caution against over-spending on computing power before the actual workflow value is proven.
  • Debated whether AI agents should be managed like software accounts or like digital employees with defined roles — an open question, not a settled one.
  • LG Electronics described a common trap: a "plain-English database search" tool that worked great in testing broke down once scaled to thousands of real tables — accuracy quietly dropped as it left the lab.
  • Closing advice: pair every AI rollout with an actual redesign of the process and people's roles — don't just bolt AI onto how things already work.
TakeawayExecutive hesitation — not data or model quality — was named as the single biggest bottleneck here. It's a leadership and incentive problem more than a technical one.
Afternoon · Track D

Panel: AI Governance in Manufacturing

Three panelists, names not captured in the recovered transcript

In short: Before any AI agent can help on the factory floor, someone has to write down the knowledge that currently only lives in people's heads.

  • Named the core difficulty as turning knowledge that lives only in experienced staff's heads into shared, written data before any AI agent can use it.

Late afternoon · Track A

From Intuition to Intelligence: How AI Has Redefined Revenue Growth 세일즈 과학의 혁명: AI는 영업을 어떻게 바꼈는가

Barra Ó Briain — Head of APEC Sales, Gong

In short: A simple, honest way to check if an AI tool is actually working: did it save time, and did it improve the actual result?

  • Cited a PwC study: 74% of AI's business value goes to just 20% of companies — not because they bought more software, but because they redesigned how they work around it, and let it make more decisions while also investing in more oversight, not less.
  • Contrasted an 88% AI-adoption figure with a separate finding that 60% of AI projects are on track to miss their goals — "everyone has the tool, almost no one has the value."
  • Proposed a simple formula for what counts as a real AI win: time saved times how much better the actual outcome is. Most companies stop at the "time saved" half.
  • From analyzing millions of real sales calls: top-performing reps talk about 46% of the time versus 68% for average reps — and they wait until much later in the call to discuss price, once real value has been shown.
  • Cited results at customer companies: roughly 10 hours per week given back to each rep, 46–50% less time to get new hires up to speed, and a self-reported 64% jump in productivity — with Anthropic named as one customer.
Takeaway"Time saved times outcome improved" is a simple, reusable check for whether an AI tool is actually working, or just automating the easy half of the job.

04

Four ideas that kept coming back

Across all 21 sessions, regardless of industry or track, these four points kept resurfacing.

01

Most AI pilots die before real use — and it's rarely the AI's fault

From the morning talk (94% saw no profit impact) to DeepMind's "80% never reach production" to the "who presses the button" problem: messy data, tools bolted onto old workflows, and hesitant leadership were named as the real blockers across five separate sessions on three different tracks.

02

The hard part moved from "which model" to "how do we actually run this"

The maturity ladder for AI tools, the $9B+ race among major vendors to embed their own engineers in customer rollouts, and the "routing tasks to the right model isn't a strategy" warning all point the same way: picking a model is the easy part now.

03

Physical AI needs a stricter safety bar than software AI

The Physical AI panel's "can it be undone?" rule, and its "test a wide range of conditions, not a fixed checklist" answer, echoed in the manufacturing panel's caution about hidden expert knowledge and in Seagate's storage-reliability framing. The moment AI acts in the physical world, a mistake stops being "a wrong answer" and becomes "an accident."

04

Good data is the prerequisite everyone agreed on, independently

ClickHouse (AI agents make unpredictable database requests), Seagate (85–95% of AI data sits on plain hard drives), DeepMind (good data is step one of four), Cisco (one shared pipeline feeds every AI use case), and the manufacturing panel ("write down the tacit knowledge first") all named the same prerequisite in different words.
