If you only have 5 minutes

The five ideas that came up again and again, in plain language.

  1. Most AI pilots never make it to real use — and it's rarely the AI's fault. Roughly 80% of AI projects never reach full production. The real blockers were messy data, tools bolted onto old workflows, and no senior person willing to approve scaling something up.
  2. Picking the AI model is the easy part now. Every major AI company (Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft, AWS) is now selling hands-on help to actually deploy AI inside a company, not just software access.
  3. Robots and physical AI need a stricter safety rule than chatbots. A chatbot's wrong answer is annoying; a robot's wrong action can hurt someone. The clearest rule offered: let AI act on its own only where a mistake can be undone.
  4. Good results always traced back to good data — not a fancier model. Storage, database, and AI companies all said the same thing in different words: organize your data first, or nothing built on top of it works reliably.
  5. The workforce most comfortable with AI is also the newest. Roughly 30% of the global office workforce is now Gen Z — training built only for senior staff will miss where AI habits are actually forming.

Six terms worth knowing before you read on

Skip this if you're already fluent in AI-conference jargon. Otherwise, these six terms show up repeatedly below.

Agentic AI — AI that doesn't just answer a question, but takes multi-step actions on its own toward a goal.
AX — shorthand for "AI Transformation," the enterprise-wide effort to adopt AI, not just a single tool.
Harness Engineering — the tooling and guardrails around an AI model so it can run unsupervised.
Sim-to-Real gap — when something tested in a lab/simulator fails once it meets real-world mess.
World model — an AI's internal understanding of how physical things behave.
FDE (Forward-Deployed Engineer) — a vendor's own engineer embedded inside a customer's rollout.

InsightThe pattern here suggests the industry conflated "more fluent" with "more capable." The deliberate constraints older assistants had weren't limitations to be discarded — they were what made a system auditable.
Vision 2030 pillarFuture Ready
TakeawayFluency isn't the same as reliability — a Future Ready test for which AI tools Benilde trusts with real responsibility, not just which ones sound impressive.

Vision 2030 Alignment: Who Makes This Real

The ideas above are only useful if someone owns turning them into action. This maps the summit's themes onto the five strategic pillars — with a specific office named against each recommendation, because the summit's own most-repeated failure mode was exactly this: no one senior owning the decision to act.

1 · Enrollment Growth

Give the Recruitment Chatbot persistent context across a prospect's full journey. Apply the reversibility test to the Academic Advising Agent now, while it's still in development.

Responsible — Center of Educational Technology (CET), with Admissions; Academic Council for advising-agent guardrails.

2 · Enhanced Reputation and Branding

Publish a first AI case study once Phase 2 pilot results land. Brand Benilde's public AI position around governance discipline, not raw capability.

Responsible — Marketing & Communications, with CET for content; Office of the Chancellor for public positioning.

3 · Future Ready

Add the three-question go/no-go gate to every Phase 2 pilot before it's proposed for scale-up. Confirm the data warehouse can handle agentic query load before the platform is selected.

Responsible — CET and Academic Council for the gate; CET / IT infrastructure for the data warehouse review.

4 · Global Partnership Engagement

Require every shortlisted platform vendor to disclose what deployment support is bundled into their contract. Scope one applied-research partnership with a lab offering free academic-tier tools.

Responsible — CET and Procurement for vendor terms; Office of the Chancellor and academic deans for research partnerships.

5 · Financial Growth

Report Phase 2 pilot results on both efficiency and effectiveness, not adoption alone. Release Phase 3 budget in gated tranches tied to Phase 2 evidence.

Responsible — CET and Finance for reporting format; the Board / Leadership Team for gated budget release.

Who leads this, overall

ownership

A single accountable office, not a rotating committee, turns the five rows above from a wish list into a plan. Proposed lead role: a Director, Office of AI-Enabled Transformation, reporting to the Office of the Chancellor, with CET as its operating arm. Decision authority: that Director applies the go/no-go gate and recommends scale-up; the Chancellor's Office holds final sign-off on anything budget-significant or reputationally sensitive.

Possible budget sources: an institutional strategic/innovation fund released in gated tranches; reallocated IT/EdTech operating budget as lower-value tools are retired; vendor co-investment (bundled deployment support); external grants/partnership funding; philanthropic or alumni giving earmarked for innovation.

06

AI Implementation: Roles & Responsibilities

The summit's clearest lesson was that roles without a decision path just recreate the "ad hoc committee" problem. Below is a proposed model for who does what, and who ultimately decides.

1. Digital Transformation Office (Program Lead)

The orchestrator, not the builder of everything.

  • Owns the roadmap's cadence and the master pilot list — the one place that can see across IT, CET, and Academic Affairs at once.
  • Runs the three-question go/no-go gate on every pilot before it's proposed for scale-up.
  • Prepares Board/BLT reporting on both efficiency and effectiveness, not adoption numbers alone.
  • Escalates anything hitting the reversibility line to Leadership — does not decide it alone.

2. IT Team

The infrastructure and safety net.

  • Owns the data-warehouse / agentic-query-capacity question before any platform is selected college-wide.
  • Runs security review on every AI vendor: data residency, access scope, breach exposure.
  • Owns uptime and support once a pilot graduates from CET's hands into daily operations.

3. Innovation & EdTech (Center of Educational Technology)

The pedagogical and use-case owner.

  • Vets tools against the EdTech AI Tools Guide rubric before anything reaches a classroom or advisor.
  • Runs the actual pilots (syllabus AI, Academic Advising Agent, Mental Health Agent) and reports results.
  • Owns faculty and student training under the AI Literacy Framework.

4. Leadership (VP for Admin & Innovation, Office of the Chancellor, BLT)

The risk owner and budget releaser — the "who presses the button" role the summit's most-repeated failure mode says usually goes unfilled.

  • Signs off on anything budget-significant or reputationally sensitive.
  • Releases Phase 3 scale-up funding in gated tranches tied to Phase 2 evidence.
  • Sets the non-negotiable boundary: which decisions, per the reversibility test, never become AI's call.

5. Other School Leaders (Academic Council, Deans/Program Chairs, Finance, Marketing & Comms, Student Affairs)

The domain implementers — a pilot that works in CET's hands still needs a Dean or Program Chair to make it standard practice.

  • Academic Council: policy guardrails for anything touching grading, advising, or academic integrity.
  • Finance: the efficiency-times-effectiveness reporting format, and sign-off on budget tranches.
  • Marketing & Communications: the public "governance discipline" narrative.
  • Student Affairs: co-owns the Mental Health Agent given its stakes.
The decision pathCET pilots → Digital Transformation Office runs the go/no-go gate → IT signs off on infrastructure/security → Leadership approves scale + budget → the domain leader owns rollout. Any step that hits the reversibility line routes to Leadership regardless of how well the pilot tested.
